Help

What do the words mean?
Your budget
All the money you can spend tonight. The budget line cuts both sides of the graph at this number.
Main cart
The things you really want to buy, and what they cost on the app.
Subjective
Depends on you. Two people can feel differently about the same item. The price is the same for everyone, but the worth is your own feeling.
Main items: subjective worth
How much those things are worth to you personally. 1.0× means worth exactly their price. 2.0× means you would happily pay double.
Charge (delivery fee and others)
Money you pay on top of your items. You can add more than one and rename each one.
Amount
How many rupees the charge costs.
Charged below
The charge applies when your cart is under this number. At this number or above, the charge goes away.
Top-up
Adding extra items only to reach that number and skip a charge.
Extras added
How many rupees of extra items you add. The smallest top-up is the least you need to skip a charge.
Extra items: subjective worth
How much the extra items are worth to you personally. 0.5× means each ₹100 of extras feels like ₹50 to you.
Net gain
What your items are subjectively worth to you, minus what you pay. Higher is better. Below zero means you lose.
Dead zone
Carts where you pay more than you would for a bigger cart. Stopping there never makes sense.
Money left
What stays in your pocket after the bill. It is the up-and-down side of the graph.
Budget line
All the carts you can afford. The jumps are where a charge disappears.
Indifference curve
All the carts that give you the same level of satisfaction, so you are equally happy with each one. A curve higher up is better. The best choice sits on the highest curve you can reach.
How do I use this page?
  1. Set your main cart and how much those items are worth to you (their subjective worth).
  2. Set the charges your app really has: the amount, and the cart value where it stops.
  3. Say how much you like the extra items.
  4. Read the best choice, and open See the calculation to check the sums.
Is this website really useful? (honest answer)

It is a good teaching tool and a small decision helper. It is not a shopping robot.

  • It helps you see why apps set a free-delivery amount, and why people add things they do not need.
  • It gives one real check before you top up: will you lose less on the extras than the charges you skip?
  • It is good for class ideas like thinking at the margin, budget lines and consumer choice.

Where it falls short:

  • The worth numbers are subjective: they are your own guesses. The page cannot know them.
  • It does not know real prices, coupons, delivery time or passes.
  • It treats all extras as one lump with the same worth per rupee. Real extras vary.
  • The prices and charges are examples, not data from any app.

To use it for real, enter your own cart and be honest about the extras. If you would not miss them, set the extra items’ subjective worth low.

Why does the budget line touch both sides at the same number?

If you spend nothing, you keep all your money. That is the top-left point, at the height of your whole budget. If you spend everything on items, you keep nothing. That is the bottom-right point, at the width of your whole budget. Both numbers are your budget, so the dashed line runs corner to corner. The solid line sits below it while charges apply, because every order pays them.

Why does the budget line have a notch?

A charge only applies to small carts. When your cart reaches the number where the charge stops, your bill suddenly gets smaller. So the money you keep suddenly gets bigger, and the line jumps up. That jump is the notch.

With the starting numbers (budget ₹200, charge ₹30 below ₹99): a ₹98 cart costs ₹128 and leaves you ₹72. A ₹99 cart costs ₹99 and leaves you ₹101. The jump is the ₹30 charge you stop paying, minus ₹1 for the extra item. The carts just before the notch are the dead zone: you pay more for less.

Why are the indifference curves bent (kinked)?

A curve joins all the carts that make you equally happy. Your main items are subjectively worth more to you than their price. So up to your main cart, each rupee of items feels big, and you would give up a lot of money to keep it. That makes the curve steep.

After your main cart, extra items are worth less than their price. You would give up less money for them, so the curve gets flatter. The bend sits exactly where your main cart ends.

Example: if main items are worth 2.0×, each ₹1 of them feels like ₹2. If extra items are worth 0.6×, each ₹1 of extras feels like 60 paise. The bend is sharp because we use only two simple numbers. Real feelings fade smoothly, so real curves would be smooth.

How are these indifference curves similar to, and different from, the classic ones?

Similar

  • Each curve joins all the carts that give you the same satisfaction, so you are indifferent between them.
  • A curve further from the corner is better.
  • The curves of one person never cross.
  • The slope shows the trade-off: how much money you would give up for a little more cart.
  • They bend toward the corner, because each extra rupee of items feels worth less to you than the one before.
  • You pick the highest curve that touches your budget line.

Different

  • A textbook graph shows two goods, such as pizza and burgers. Here one side is the cart and the other side is money left. Economists often use money left to stand for everything else you could buy.
  • A textbook curve is smooth, because the feeling fades a little at every step. Ours has two straight pieces and one bend, because we use only two subjective worth numbers: one for main items and one for extra items. It is like a smooth hill drawn with two ramps.
  • A textbook budget line is straight. Ours has a notch where a charge ends, so the best choice can sit at the corner of the notch instead of at a smooth touching point.
  • Real feelings are smooth, so a real curve would have no sharp bend. We use two numbers so you can type them and check every sum by hand.
Why does the graph show money left?

A charge buys you nothing, so it cannot be one of the things on the graph. It only cuts the money you keep. So the graph shows the cart on one side and the money you keep on the other.
